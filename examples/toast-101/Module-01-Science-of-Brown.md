## Lesson

### Why toast turns brown
Put a slice of white bread in a toaster for 30 seconds and it comes out warm but pale. Leave it for 2–3 minutes and it comes out golden, smells nutty, and tastes completely different. Nothing was added, so the bread must have changed itself.

That change is mostly the **Maillard reaction**: amino acids (from the bread's proteins) react with reducing sugars (from its starch) under dry heat. The reaction produces hundreds of new flavor and aroma compounds, plus brown pigments called **melanoidins**. It speeds up a lot once the surface gets hotter than about 140 °C.

> **Analogy:** think of it as a party that only starts when the room is hot enough. Below about 140 °C the guests (sugars and amino acids) are there but barely mingle. Above it, they pair up fast and every pairing leaves something new behind: flavor, smell, color.

### Maillard vs. caramelization
Both reactions make food brown, so they get confused.

| | Maillard reaction | Caramelization |
|---|---|---|
| Needs | Sugars **and** amino acids | Sugars only |
| Starts noticeably | About 140 °C | About 160 °C for table sugar (lower for some sugars, e.g. fructose) |
| Tastes like | Toasty, savory, nutty | Sweet, buttery, slightly bitter |
| Main role in toast | Most of the color and flavor | A small part, mainly in sweet breads |

Plain bread has little free sugar, so toast is mostly Maillard. A sugary brioche browns faster and darker because caramelization joins in.

### First it dries, then it browns
Bread is roughly 35–40% water. Water can't get hotter than 100 °C at normal pressure, so while the surface is wet it stays stuck near 100 °C, well below Maillard temperatures. The first part of toasting is just **drying the surface**. Browning starts only after that.

This explains two things you've probably seen:
- **Fresh, moist bread takes longer to toast** than day-old bread.
- **Toast is crisp outside and soft inside:** the surface dries and browns, while the center keeps its water.

## Practice
*Maps to: Weekly toast log 1*
1. [O1] Toast one slice of white bread on the lowest setting and one on a medium setting. Describe the color and smell of each in one sentence.
2. [O2] Toast a slice of plain white bread and a slice of a sweet bread (brioche, or raisin bread) on the same setting. Which is darker? Explain why using the table above.
3. [O3] Toast a slice of fresh bread and a slice left out uncovered for a day, on the same setting. Which browns first, and why?
4. [O4] Predict: what happens if you toast a slice straight from the freezer on your usual setting? Test it.

**Mini-project (optional):** make a "shade card" by toasting 5 slices on settings 1 through 5. Photograph them in a row and label each one. You'll reuse this card in Module 4.

<details><summary>Solutions / expected results</summary>

1. The low-setting slice is warm and pale and smells like bread. The medium slice is golden, smells nutty or toasty, and is crisp on the surface.
2. The sweet bread is darker. It has more free sugar, so caramelization adds to the Maillard browning.
3. The day-old slice browns first. It has less surface water to drive off before the temperature can pass 100 °C.
4. It comes out paler than usual. The ice has to melt and evaporate first, which takes up most of the toasting time. (This is why some toasters have a "frozen" button that adds time.)
</details>

## Quiz
1. [O1] Which two kinds of molecules react in the Maillard reaction?
2. [O2] A slice of plain white bread browns mainly because of: (a) caramelization, (b) the Maillard reaction, (c) burning.
3. [O3] Why can't a wet bread surface brown?
4. [O4] Why does brioche brown faster than plain white bread on the same setting?
5. [O3] Why is toast crisp outside but soft inside?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Amino acids and reducing sugars.** A common wrong answer is "sugar and heat": heat drives the reaction but isn't one of the molecules.
2. **(b) the Maillard reaction.** Plain bread has little free sugar, so caramelization plays only a small part. Burning comes later, when the surface turns black and bitter.
3. **Water holds the surface near 100 °C**, well below the roughly 140 °C where Maillard browning speeds up. The water has to evaporate first.
4. **Brioche has more sugar**, so caramelization adds to the Maillard browning.
5. **Only the surface dries out and browns.** The center keeps its water and stays near 100 °C.
</details>
